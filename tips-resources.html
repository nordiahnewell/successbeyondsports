Taking care of yourself

Mental Wellness Tips & Strategies

Always prioritize your mental health - Your wellbeing is just as important as your performance in school and academics, taking care of yourself can help you succeed in both.

Getting Enough Sleep

Try to aim to sleep with 8-10 hours.

Taking care of your body

Eating healthy and balanced foods.

Taking breaks

Taking a break away from everything and taking deep breaths can help you recharge.

Building a support team

Building a support team and surrounding yourself with people who encourage you and support all of your pathways.

Practicing Self Care

Creating time to do things you enjoy and finding a routine to relax your mind.

Balancing sports and academics isn't always about being perfect, finding things that works best for you to help you succeed is what the most important part is. Whether that's setting goals or taking breaks, every student athlete works differently and their paths are a lot different. Most importantly, don't forget to ask for help from someone you trust is a key way to succeed.

Study smarter, not just longer

Effective Study Techniques

When practice eats into your evenings, how you study matters as much as how long you study.

Study in short, focused blocks

Long unstructured study sessions are hard to fit around a practice schedule, and hard to focus through even when you have the time. Try 25–30 minute focused blocks with short breaks in between — it's easier to slot in around training and easier to actually concentrate through.

Practice active recall

Instead of re-reading notes, close them and try to explain the material out loud or write it from memory. It takes less time than re-reading and sticks far better — useful when your study windows are short.

  • Turn your notes into quiz questions and test yourself
  • Teach the material to a teammate or sibling — if you can explain it, you know it
  • Use flashcards for anything you need to memorize on the go, like on the bus to a game

Front-load work before busy stretches

If you know a tournament or playoff run is coming, look ahead at your assignments and start early pieces of bigger projects before the schedule gets tight, rather than trying to catch up after.

Protect one consistent study window

Even 20–30 minutes at the same time each day builds a habit that's easier to stick to than trying to "find time" fresh every day.

You don't have to figure it out alone

Networking & Mentorship

The right mentor or connection can open doors that are hard to find on your own — in sport, school, and beyond.

Why mentorship matters

A mentor — a coach, teacher, older student, or professional in a field you're curious about — can offer guidance, honest feedback, and connections that speed up your growth, both academically and athletically. Many successful student-athletes point to one adult who believed in them early on and helped them see a path forward.

Where to find mentors

  • Coaches and teachers you already trust — ask them directly if they'd be open to mentoring you
  • School guidance counsellors, who often know about mentorship or leadership programs
  • Community organizations focused on Black youth (see the Resources section above)
  • Alumni from your school who went on to play or study in an area you're interested in

Building your network

Networking doesn't have to feel transactional. Showing up consistently, asking genuine questions, and following up after a conversation (even a short thank-you message) goes a long way. Teammates, coaches from other schools, and people you meet at tournaments or academic events can all become part of a network that supports you long after high school.

Success isn't achieved alone

Community Support & Involvement

Being part of a community — in sport and beyond — gives you people to lean on when school and sport both get demanding.

Why community matters

Student-athletes who feel connected to a broader community — teammates, cultural or faith groups, neighbourhood programs — tend to report better handling the stress of balancing school and sport. Community gives you people who understand what you're going through and can offer real support, not just advice.

Ways to get involved

  • Join or stay connected to programs built specifically for Black youth in your city
  • Volunteer or mentor younger students — teaching others is one of the best ways to solidify your own growth
  • Join clubs or affinity groups at your school, not just sports teams
  • Attend community events tied to your sport, like clinics or fundraisers, where you'll meet people outside your usual circle

It goes both ways

Community support isn't just about receiving help — showing up for others in your community (teammates, younger athletes, classmates) builds the kind of relationships that support you right back when you need it.

Fuel for two full-time jobs

Nutrition & Fitness

Being a student-athlete is basically two jobs — your body and brain need real fuel to keep up with both.

Eat to support both practice and studying

Skipping meals or relying on whatever's fastest between school and practice catches up with you — in energy on the field and focus in class. Balanced meals with protein, whole grains, fruits, and vegetables help sustain energy through a long day rather than causing crashes.

Simple habits that help

  • Eat something with protein and carbs within an hour or two before practice or games
  • Keep easy snacks on hand for the gap between school and practice — fruit, nuts, yogurt
  • Stay hydrated throughout the day, not just during practice
  • Don't skip breakfast on game days or test days — your brain needs fuel too

Movement beyond your sport

Light activity like stretching, walking, or mobility work on rest days helps recovery and can actually improve focus for studying. Rest days are part of training, not a break from it — treat them that way.

This is general guidance, not individual medical or nutrition advice — talk to a coach, trainer, or healthcare provider for anything specific to you.

It's okay to not have it all figured out

Coping with Stress & Anxiety

Between tests, games, and everything in between, stress is going to show up. What matters is having healthy ways to move through it.

Notice it early

Trouble sleeping, irritability, losing interest in things you usually enjoy, or feeling constantly on edge can all be signs that stress is building up. Catching it early makes it much easier to manage than waiting until it feels overwhelming.

Healthy ways to cope

  • Talk to someone you trust — a friend, parent, coach, or counsellor — before it piles up
  • Break big worries into smaller, specific problems you can actually act on
  • Use movement intentionally — sometimes a workout helps, sometimes a slow walk does more
  • Write things down. Getting a racing mind onto paper can take some of its weight away
  • Give yourself permission to rest without guilt — rest is part of performing well, not a failure to keep up

When to ask for more support

If stress or anxiety is sticking around, affecting your sleep, appetite, or ability to function day to day, that's worth talking to a professional about — a school counsellor, doctor, or a service like Kids Help Phone (see the Resources section above). Asking for help is a sign of strength, not weakness.
